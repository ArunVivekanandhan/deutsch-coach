⚡ Past Tense Cheat Codes & Quick Hacks (German Grammar)

Shortcut 1: The "English Cousin" Rule (Cognates)
Because English and German are historic cousins, strong verbs in English are almost always strong verbs in German.
Ask yourself what the verb does in English past tense:
  • stehen = to stand → English: stood (vowel change) → German stehen is strong (stand, gestanden).
  • singen = to sing → English: sang, sung (irregular) → German singen is strong (sang, gesungen).
  • trinken = to drink → English: drank, drunk (irregular) → German trinken is strong (trank, getrunken).
  • hören = to hear → English: heard (regular -d ending) → German hören is weak (hörte, gehört).
Shortcut 2: The Double-Vowel (Diphthong) Rule
Look at the vowel combinations in the basic infinitive form. German verbs with certain vowel combinations are almost always strong:
  • -ei- verbs are 99% strong! They flip to -ie-: bleiben → blieb, schreiben → schrieb, leihen → lieh.
  • -ie- verbs are 99% strong! They flip to -o-: fliegen → flog, bieten → bot, schließen → schloss.
Formula: -ei- → -ie- | -ie- → -o-
Shortcut 3: The "Caveman" Rule (Ancient Core Life Verbs)
Strong verbs are thousands of years old. If a caveman did it to survive, it is almost certainly a strong verb:
  • Eat (essen → aß), Drink (trinken → trank), Sleep (schlafen → schlief), Run (laufen → lief), Die (sterben → starb), Give (geben → gab).
  • Modern/social activities are weak: surfen, chatten, fotografieren, kündigen, reservieren (weak -te endings).
Shortcut 4: Weak vs. Strong Verb Formulas
1. Regular Weak Verbs (90% of German): Stem + -te (Präteritum) | ge- + Stem + -(e)t (Perfekt)
2. Strong Verbs (Ablautreihen): Vowel Change in Stem (no -t-) | ge- + Vowel-Changed Stem + -en
machen → machte → hat gemacht | singen → sang → hat gesungen
Shortcut 5: Inseparable Prefix NO-ge Rule (be-, ge-, emp-, ent-, er-, ver-, zer-)
Verbs with inseparable prefixes NEVER take ge- in Perfekt!
besuchen → hat besucht (NOT hat gebesucht) | verstehen → hat verstanden
Shortcut 6: Trennbare Verben "ge-" Sandwich Rule
Separable prefixes split in present & simple past, and sandwich -ge- in the middle for Perfekt:
einkaufen → kaufte ein → hat ein-ge-kauft | anrufen → rief an → hat an-ge-rufen
Shortcut 7: Spoken German (Perfekt) vs. Written (Präteritum)
In spoken German (A1-B1), always use Perfekt (haben/sein + Partizip II). Use Präteritum primarily for sein (war), haben (hatte), werden (wurde), and modal verbs (musste, konnte, wollte, durfte, sollte).

🧩 Compound Words & Punarchi (Glue Letters) Rules

1. The "Right-Side Boss" Rule (இறுதிச் சொல் விதி)
When German words are glued together into a compound word (Kompositum), the very last word on the right determines the main meaning and the gender (der/die/das) of the entire new word. Everything to the left just describes it!

Joined Word Splitting (பிரித்து எழுதுக) Literal Meaning Actual Meaning
der Handschuh Hand + der Schuh Hand + Shoe Glove (கையுறை)
der Kühlschrank Kühlen + der Schrank Cool + Closet Refrigerator (குளிர்சாதனப் பெட்டி)
das Wörterbuch Wörter + das Buch Words + Book Dictionary (அகராதி)
2. "Glue Letters" (The German Punarchi / புணர்ச்சி விதி)
In Tamil, when joining words (புணர்ச்சி / Punarchi), extra letters often appear in the middle to make pronunciation flow better (like adding க், ச், த், ப் — e.g. தமிழ் + தாய் = தமிழ்த்தாய்).

German does the exact same thing using "glue letters" (Fugenlaute), most commonly adding an -s-, -n-, or -er- between the words:
  • Arbeitsplatz (Workplace) = Arbeit (Work) + s (glue letter) + Platz (Place). Gender: der Platz → der Arbeitsplatz.
  • Geburtstag (Birthday) = Geburt (Birth) + s (glue letter) + Tag (Day). Gender: der Tag → der Geburtstag.
  • Straßenbahn (Tram) = Straße (Street) + n (glue letter) + Bahn (Train). Gender: die Bahn → die Straßenbahn.
  • Bilderbuch (Picture book) = Bild (Picture) + er (glue letter) + Buch (Book). Gender: das Buch → das Bilderbuch.
